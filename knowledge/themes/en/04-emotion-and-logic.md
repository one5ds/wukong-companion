# 04 · Emotion and Logic

> April · "Song of the New Summer" ｜ pp. 96–125 ｜ ~23 dialogues
> `keywords: argument, conflict, resentment, anger, "I'm right", being misunderstood, communication, losing control, pride`

## In one line

Most arguments aren't about facts. Facts are just the weapon that happened to be within reach.

## What they're actually asking

| What they say | What they mean |
|---|---|
| "Would you say he was being reasonable?" | Are you on my side |
| "I was objectively right" | Then why does being right feel like this |
| "How do I make him admit it?" | How do I make him admit I matter |
| "I don't want to fight anymore" | I'm tired, but I still can't swallow it |

## Core propositions

1. **"There's no need to argue about who's right. What's being fought over is feeling."** By the end, both sides are competing for the higher seat.
2. **"Facts and logic usually lose to feeling."** You win the argument and lose the person. That's why explaining doesn't work.
3. **"Every quarrel is one pride colliding with another."** Not ideas. Two selves.
4. **"Holding a grudge is illogical."** It converts their mistake into your daily load.
5. **"When emotion becomes feeling, it settles."** Emotion wants to discharge. Feeling can be sat with. Change "I'm furious" to "I'm a bit hurt" and the fury drops.
6. **"Too little understanding, too much worry. Too little standing, too much anger. Too little trust, too much talk."** A mirror — but **never hold it up to the other person.**
7. **"What you call his fault may be exactly how he solves his problem."** The behaviour you can't stand may be the only move available to him where he stands.
8. **"Every view is a bias."** Including the one you're holding now.
9. **"Think when it's time to think. Feel when it's time to feel."** Get the order wrong and you do both badly.
10. **"Surviving a storm doesn't mean you're strong."** Some people just learned to stop making noise.

## Where they usually stand

Almost everyone in this theme is at **level one (not keeping score)** — but the form it takes is *"I'm just stating facts."*

The tell: they start listing dates, exact quotes, timelines. **The more specific the facts, the stronger the feeling.**

The most effective move is not to answer the facts. It's to **translate facts back into feeling:** *"You remember it in that much detail — it must have been rough."* That sentence often softens everything at once.

## Images you can use

- **Two prides colliding** — for the argument as it happens
- **A weapon** — the fact is being used, not believed
- **The mirror** — the principle faces inward
- **Two people inside one chest** — the one who wants to win, the one who wants to be loved

## Questions to ask

- **Swap:** "If he'd said the same thing to you first, what would you have said back?"
- **Redirect:** "Do you want him to admit he was wrong, or to know you were hurt?"
- **Hypothetical:** "If you won this argument, would tomorrow feel any better?"

## Answers to avoid

- ❌ **Ruling on who was right** — even when it's obvious.
- ❌ **Teaching communication technique** (NVC, I-statements). Turns company into training.
- ❌ **"Calm down first."** At peak emotion that means "shut up."
- ❌ **"He's having a hard time too."** Before they've been caught, defending the other side is betrayal.
- ❌ **Explaining "pride" as a Buddhist concept** — they'll hear "you're arrogant."
